# AI-shaped writing patterns

## Purpose

This guide helps identify prose that feels generic, inflated, repetitive, or templated. It is a diagnostic aid, not an AI detector. A single pattern is not proof that text was generated by AI or that it needs editing.

## Common patterns and repairs

### 1. Ceremonial openings

**Pattern:** “Great question. Let's explore this fascinating topic.”

**Repair:** Start with the actual answer or issue.

### 2. Importance without information

**Pattern:** “This marks a pivotal moment in the evolving landscape.”

**Repair:** Name the actual change, event, consequence, or remove the claim.

### 3. Generic claims with vague attribution

**Pattern:** “Experts believe this plays a crucial role.”

**Repair:** Cite a specific source when available, state the factual claim with proper scope, or omit the unsupported attribution.

### 4. Forced contrast

**Pattern:** “It's not just X; it's Y.” Repeated contrasts can inflate an ordinary point.

**Repair:** State the actual distinction directly. Keep a contrast when it is genuinely central to the idea.

### 5. Artificial triads and parallel lists

**Pattern:** “Innovation, inspiration, and impact” regardless of what the content requires.

**Repair:** Use the number and shape of points the argument actually needs. Three items are fine when there are three real items.

### 6. Synonym cycling

**Pattern:** Repeatedly switching between “model”, “system”, “solution”, “platform”, and “tool” for the same object.

**Repair:** Reuse the precise term consistently.

### 7. Abstract nouns in place of actions

**Pattern:** “Leverage the implementation of an optimization initiative.”

**Repair:** Name the action and actor: “The team will optimize the query.” Keep formal terminology when it has a specific technical meaning.

### 8. Formulaic transitions and signposting

**Pattern:** “Moreover”, “Additionally”, “Furthermore”, “In conclusion” at predictable intervals.

**Repair:** Use a direct transition, a meaningful connective, or no transition.

### 9. Redundant section closers

**Pattern:** A paragraph gives an example and then adds “This highlights the importance of...” without adding a conclusion.

**Repair:** Remove the repetition or state the actual implication.

### 10. Excessive formatting

**Pattern:** Every sentence has a bold label; every answer has a table and a summary box.

**Repair:** Choose the lightest format that makes the response easier to understand.

### 11. Overly polished false certainty

**Pattern:** An unsupported recommendation is stated as unquestionably correct.

**Repair:** State the evidence, assumptions, and uncertainty accurately. Do not insert hedging where the evidence is strong, either.

### 12. Generic service language

**Pattern:** “I hope this helps. Feel free to reach out if you have further questions.”

**Repair:** End when the user has what they asked for.

### 13. Overuse of em dashes or sentence fragments

**Pattern:** Repeated dramatic pauses and fragments used as a default rhythm.

**Repair:** Edit where the punctuation or fragment makes the passage theatrical or hard to follow. Do not ban em dashes, semicolons, fragments, or any other form universally.

### 14. Fake personal texture

**Pattern:** Invented anecdotes, “I remember when...”, claims of personal struggle, or invented first-hand testing.

**Repair:** Remove fabricated experience. Use an example explicitly marked as hypothetical if one helps explain the point.

### 15. Generic conclusion

**Pattern:** “The future looks bright, and exciting times lie ahead.”

**Repair:** State the real implication, concrete next step, or stop without a conclusion.

## Contextual exceptions

A pattern is a signal, not a defect. Leave it alone inside quotations, titles, proper names, and passages that discuss the pattern. A list of three is correct when there are three items. A contrast is correct when the distinction is the point. Em dashes, fragments, and semicolons are fine when they help. Formal salutations and sign-offs are normal in letters and emails. Word lists change between model generations; judge by effect on the reader, not by vocabulary.

## Review procedure

Apply these patterns while drafting so that most answers need no second pass. Use the steps below when revising a draft or the user's text (see `revising-text.md`).

1. Read the whole answer before editing.
2. Identify the actual point and the audience.
3. Mark only patterns that weaken this particular answer.
4. Revise toward specificity, logical flow, and authentic register. Do not merely swap one cliché for another.
5. Compare the revision against the source to ensure no fact, qualification, promise, or useful nuance was lost.
6. If the original already reads well, leave it alone.

## Do not optimize for detector evasion

The objective is communication quality. Do not add mistakes, unnatural variation, misleading anecdotes, or random stylistic quirks to manipulate AI-detection tools.
